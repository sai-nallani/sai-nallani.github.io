---
title: "Coordinate Frames, Robot Learning, and Gravity"
date: "2026-10-05"
---

This summer at Princeton, I worked on canonicalization for robot manipulation: expressing a scene in a consistent coordinate frame so a robot can reuse what it learned when objects move. Earlier work explored SE(3), the group of three-dimensional rotations and translations, but found only modest performance gains. That motivated our question: when does this extra symmetry actually help a robot learn to manipulate objects?

We explored using the gripper itself as the reference frame, expressing both the point-cloud observations and the robot's target motions relative to it. We trained on ten simulated LIBERO tasks with 50 demonstrations per task. Under combined yaw rotation and a 10 cm horizontal shift, the world-coordinate policy succeeded in **0/679** valid trials, our SE(3) gripper policy in **390/679 (57.4%)**, and the planar scene-frame policy in **478/679 (70.4%)**. Canonicalization helped substantially, but the scene frame still led by **13.0 percentage points**. These were planar tests with one training run per task and unpaired evaluation randomness across methods, not evidence of robustness to arbitrary 3D rotations.

A paired control on one task suggested that coupling observation and action frames matters: coupled gripper coordinates achieved **69/72 successes (95.8%)**, compared with **47/72 (65.3%)** for a planar gripper control. The gain was **30.6 percentage points**, with a 95% interval of **12.5–48.6 points** from bootstrapping the 12 starting layouts. This result is conditional on that trained pair, rather than a claim about every task.

One possible explanation for the gap is gravity. A manipulation task is not physically unchanged by every 3D rotation: gravity singles out a vertical direction. In world coordinates, “down” is fixed; after canonicalization, its coordinates change with the reference frame. Without that information, a policy may lose a useful physical cue. In a separate paired pilot, adding gravity features gave **37/72 successes (51.4%)**, versus **45/72 (62.5%)** without them. That showed no clear benefit, so gravity remains a hypothesis rather than an explanation we established.
