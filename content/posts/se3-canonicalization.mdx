## The question

If a robot learns to pick up a bowl, moving the bowl should not require learning the motion from scratch. *Canonicalization* expresses inputs and outputs in a consistent reference frame; [Dym, Lawrence, and Siegel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16077) give a formal account of this approach and its limitations. Earlier internal experiments motivating this project had found only modest gains from SE(3), the group of 3D rotations and translations. During summer 2026 at Princeton, advised by Christine Allen-Blanchette, I investigated whether the choice of frame—and how a policy represents its actions—could explain the gap.

I implemented gripper-frame policy variants and controls, worked on the data, training, and rollout pipeline, and ran the comparisons below. A ten-task result prompted a more focused experiment: keeping the observation representation fixed while changing the action coordinates raised success from 65.3% to 93.1% on one task.

## Setup

I adapted [PointFlowMatch](https://pointflowmatch.cs.uni-freiburg.de/): a PointNet point-cloud encoder conditions a 1D U-Net that generates action sequences through flow matching. Each of ten [LIBERO Spatial](https://libero-project.github.io/main.html) tasks—a benchmark of simulated robot manipulation—used 50 demonstrations and 512-point observations from two cameras. The robot executes one action before replanning.

| Frame | What it follows | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| World | Fixed simulator axes | Ten-task baseline |
| Planar scene | Initial object layout; fixed for the episode | Ten-task scene baseline |
| SE(3) gripper | Hand position and full 3D orientation | Ten-task gripper policy; full-gripper action targets in the control |
| Planar gripper | Hand's horizontal position and yaw; world height and tilt remain visible | Observations in every control; action targets in its baseline |

## First: a gap across ten tasks

The gripper-frame policy improved substantially over world coordinates, but trailed the simpler planar scene frame:

| Policy frame | Successes | Success rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| World | 0/679 | 0.0% |
| SE(3) gripper | 390/679 | 57.4% |
| Planar scene | 478/679 | 70.4% |

These tests combined yaw angles of ±10°, ±20°, and ±30° with a +10 cm horizontal shift. Ten tasks × 12 starting layouts × six angles gave 720 attempted trials per policy. Of these, 679 passed the setup checks: hand placement within 1 cm of its transformed start, and object drift/height deviation within 3 cm. The other 41 were rejected before policy rollout.

The 13.0-point gap made me question whether canonicalizing everything relative to the hand was the right choice. There was one training run per task, and evaluation did not use the same random seeds across methods. These planar tests also cannot establish robustness to arbitrary 3D rotations.

## Then: isolate the action coordinates

To investigate, I focused on LIBERO's task of picking up the black bowl on the cookie box and placing it on the plate. I kept observations in the planar gripper frame and changed the action targets to use the full hand pose, including height and tilt. Instead of representing targets in upright axes, the policy can describe a motion along the hand's own tilted axis.

| Action targets | Successes | Success rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Planar gripper | 47/72 | 65.3% |
| Full gripper, uncoupled | 67/72 | 93.1% |
| Full gripper, coupled | 69/72 | 95.8% |

Every policy faced the same 12 starting layouts, six yaw angles, +10 cm shift, and evaluation random seeds. The more focused comparison is the first two rows: **+27.8 percentage points**, with a 95% interval of **9.7–45.8**.

The third row asks a follow-up question. During training, I randomly perturb the observation frame. The *coupled* variant applies that same perturbation to the full-gripper action frame; the uncoupled variant leaves it unperturbed. This added only 2.8 points, with an interval of −6.9–16.7: no clear additional benefit.

![Action-coordinate change: plus 27.8 percentage points, 95% interval 9.7 to 45.8. Additional training-frame coupling: plus 2.8 points, interval minus 6.9 to 16.7.](/images/se3-frame-controls.svg)

*The 95% intervals resample the 12 starting layouts, keeping each layout's six outcomes together. They describe these trained policies, not variation across training seeds.*

The tension remains: full-gripper actions helped on this bowl task, yet the full-SE(3) gripper policy trailed across ten tasks. The control retains planar observations and expresses all targets relative to the current hand; the original gripper policy uses full-SE(3) observations and step-by-step targets. The control identifies a promising design choice, not an explanation of the entire ten-task gap.

## Gravity and the next experiment

Gravity is another possible explanation. “Down” is fixed in world coordinates but changes coordinates in a full gripper frame. In a separate pilot on the same cookie-box-to-plate task, I compared the original SE(3) gripper policy with step-by-step targets, with and without explicit gravity features. The policies shared 72 evaluation trials and random seeds: 37/72 successes (51.4%) with gravity, versus 45/72 (62.5%) without. That 62.5% belongs to this separate full-SE(3) policy comparison, not the planar-gripper control above. Gravity features did not provide a fix.

The next experiment would repeat the action-coordinate control across tasks and training seeds, then compare gripper and scene frames with matched full-SE(3) parameterizations. I wrapped up with the [code, raw results, and handoff notes](https://github.com/sai-nallani/se3-canonicalization) available for someone to continue the work.

Related work: [Wang et al.'s local-canonicalization policies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14431) study planar swarm control, a different setting from the single-arm manipulation experiments here.
